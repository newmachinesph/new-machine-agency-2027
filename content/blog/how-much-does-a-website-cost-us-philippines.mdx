Ask five agencies for a website quote and you'll get five wildly different numbers. None of them are necessarily wrong. They're just answering different questions, each imagining a slightly different project behind the same three words, "build a website." Here's how to make sense of it, whether you're buying in dollars or in pesos.

The goal isn't to find the cheapest quote. It's to understand what you're actually paying for, so you can tell a fair price from an inflated one, and a bargain from a trap.

## The three things that actually drive price

**Scope.** A one-page landing page and a 20-page corporate website are different projects, even if both get called "a website." Every page you add means more design, more copy, and more testing, and those costs stack quietly. The fastest way to lower a quote is almost never to haggle. It's to launch with fewer, better pages and add the rest once the site is earning its keep.

**Custom versus template.** Template sites are cheap because most of the work is already done, and it usually shows the moment a visitor senses they've seen this layout somewhere before. Custom design costs more because someone is solving *your* problem instead of reselling a generic one. For most growing businesses, the middle path is the sensible one: custom design built on a proven technical foundation, rather than either extreme.

**Who's accountable after launch.** A freelancer who disappears after handover is cheaper than a partner who answers the phone in launch week when something breaks at the worst possible moment. Part of what you pay an agency for is that someone is still there in month six, when a plugin update takes the contact form down and you need it fixed today, not eventually.

## Typical ranges you'll see

**In the US:**

- **DIY builders** (Wix, Squarespace): the cost of your own evenings and weekends. Perfectly fine for testing an idea before you invest real money.
- **Freelancer template builds:** low thousands of dollars. Quality varies enormously here, so ask to see three live sites they actually built, not a portfolio of mockups.
- **Professional custom websites:** mid four figures to low five figures, depending on scope. This is where most established SMEs and funded startups should be, and where the price tends to pay for itself.
- **Enterprise builds:** six figures. You'll know if you genuinely need this, and most businesses never will, whatever a sales deck tries to tell them.

**In the Philippines:**

- **DIY builders:** the same trade as anywhere else, your time instead of your money.
- **Freelancer template builds:** roughly ₱15,000 to ₱60,000. The same caveat applies: ask for three live sites, not mockups, and check they still load.
- **Professional custom websites:** low to mid six figures in pesos, depending on scope. Established SMEs competing in Metro Manila or selling abroad usually land here.
- **Enterprise builds:** seven figures and up, the territory of large corporates, banks, and conglomerates. Most businesses never need this tier and shouldn't be talked into it.

The gap between the two markets is real, but be careful about reading it as a straight discount. A strong Manila-based team building to international standards will quote closer to international rates, because the work, and the results it produces, are the same. What you're often buying in the Philippines isn't cheaper work, it's the same standard of work without the overhead of a large agency layered on top.

## Questions that expose a bad quote

1. **"What exactly is included?"** If the answer is vague, the scope will quietly grow, and so will the invoice, usually right when you have the least room to argue.
2. **"Who writes the content?"** Copy is the most commonly forgotten line item, and a beautiful site with placeholder text converts nobody. Find out before you sign, not after.
3. **"Do I own everything?"** Domain, code, and content should all be yours. If the answer isn't an immediate, unambiguous yes, treat it as a reason to walk away.
4. **"What happens after launch?"** Support terms should be in writing before you pay a single dollar or peso, because "we'll sort it out later" almost always means you'll sort it out alone.

## The real question isn't cost, it's return

A $6,000 site that brings you two new clients a month is cheap by any honest measure. An $800 template that visitors bounce off is expensive at any price, and the same math holds in pesos. The number at the bottom of the quote tells you what the site costs. It tells you nothing about what it's worth.

Judge quotes by what the site is built to *do*: whether it loads fast, speaks to the right buyer, and gives that buyer an obvious next step. A slightly higher price that reliably produces inquiries is the cheaper option in the end.
