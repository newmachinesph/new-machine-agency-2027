Your website never complains, never calls in sick, and never tells you when it's losing you business. It just quietly underperforms while you assume everything is fine. These are the five most common leaks we find when auditing SME websites, and the good news is that every one of them is fixable without starting over.

Read each sign as a question about your own site. If you're not sure of the answer, that uncertainty is usually the first clue that something is leaking.

## 1. It takes more than three seconds to load

More than half of mobile visitors abandon a page that takes over three seconds to appear. They don't file a complaint. They tap back and call your competitor, and you never even know they were there. A slow site doesn't announce the sale it lost; it simply fails to make it.

Test your site right now on your phone, using mobile data rather than office Wi-Fi, because your customers aren't sitting on your fast connection. If you have time to wonder whether it's working, so do they. The usual culprits are oversized images, a bloated template stuffed with features you never use, and cheap hosting that buckles the moment two people visit at once. Any one of those is worth fixing on its own.

## 2. There's no obvious next step

Land on your homepage and ask yourself one honest question: *what am I supposed to do here?* If the answer isn't visible without scrolling, whether that's a clear button, a phone number, or a booking link, visitors will read, nod, and leave without ever acting.

Every page needs one obvious action. Not five competing options, which force the visitor to make a decision and freeze instead. One. The business that makes the next step effortless almost always wins over the one that makes visitors hunt for it, even when the second business is better at the actual work.

## 3. It looks fine on your laptop and broken on a phone

Most of your visitors are on a phone, often checking you out during a spare minute between other things. If your menu is fiddly, your text is tiny, or your forms require pinch-zooming to fill in, you're effectively closed for the majority of your audience.

"Not broken on mobile" isn't the bar. *Designed for mobile* is. That means tap targets big enough for a thumb, text you can read without zooming, and forms short enough to finish one-handed at a bus stop. A site that merely survives on a small screen still loses to one that was built for it.

## 4. Your last update was two years ago

An outdated copyright year, a "news" section frozen in 2023, a service you stopped offering months ago but still list front and centre. Small things individually, but visitors notice all of them, and together they leave a quiet impression that the lights are off.

They ask themselves a fair question: *if they don't maintain their own website, how will they treat my project?* Freshness is a trust signal, and staleness is the opposite. You don't need to publish constantly. You do need the basics to be current, and you need the site to look like a business that's still very much open.

## 5. You can't answer "how many inquiries came from the site last month?"

If you don't know, the honest answer is probably "fewer than you think." A website without analytics and inquiry tracking isn't an asset. It's a brochure you can't measure, and a brochure can't tell you whether it's working or where exactly it's failing.

You don't need a dashboard with forty charts you'll never open. You need one number you check every month: inquiries from the site, and whether that number is climbing or sliding. Once you can see it, you can improve it. Until you can see it, you're guessing, and guessing is the most expensive way to run a website.

## The fix usually isn't a full rebuild

Sometimes it is. But more often, two or three targeted changes recover most of the lost conversions: a faster load, a clearer call to action, a mobile-first cleanup of the pages that matter most.
