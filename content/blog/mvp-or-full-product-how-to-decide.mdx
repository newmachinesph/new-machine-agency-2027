Every founder we talk to has heard "just build an MVP" so many times that the phrase has stopped meaning anything. In practice it gets stretched to justify two opposite mistakes: shipping something so thin it can't prove the idea works, or calling a six-month build an "MVP" because the word makes the scope feel smaller and safer than it really is. Neither one helps you. One wastes your launch, the other wastes your runway, and both leave you no wiser than before. Here's how we actually decide, engagement by engagement.

## Start with the question you're trying to answer

An MVP isn't a smaller product. It's an experiment, and every experiment exists to answer a question. Before scoping a single feature, write down the specific question the build needs to answer: *Will clinics pay for automated booking reminders? Will restaurant owners actually upload their own menu photos, or will we end up doing it for them every time?* If you can't state that question in one plain sentence, you're not ready to scope an MVP yet. You're guessing at features and hoping one of them lands.

This matters because the question quietly decides what you build. If the question is "will anyone pay," you need a real payment flow and a real price, because a fake checkout tells you nothing about whether someone will part with money. If the question is "does this workflow even make sense," a clickable prototype might be plenty, and you don't need any working infrastructure behind it yet. Same product on paper, completely different builds, and the question is what separates them.

## The signs you actually need a full product

Skip the MVP conversation entirely when one of these is true:

- **You already have validated demand.** If you've pre-sold the product, have a waitlist of paying customers, or are simply replacing a manual process your own team already runs every day, you're not testing whether people want it. You already know they do. Stop validating and ship the real thing.
- **Trust is the product.** Healthcare, finance, anything handling sensitive data or money. A flimsy MVP that mishandles a booking or a payment doesn't just fail to validate the idea. It burns the exact trust you'll need for the real launch, and that trust is far harder to win back than it was to earn the first time.
- **The market only gives you one shot.** Some categories are unforgiving of a rough first impression, enterprise buyers evaluating vendors being the classic example. A visibly unfinished product can disqualify you in the first five minutes, before the idea itself ever gets judged on its merits.

## The signs an MVP is the right call

- **You genuinely don't know if people want this.** The core assumption is still unproven, and building the full version first means betting six months of work on a guess you haven't tested.
- **The market is easy to re-enter if you're wrong.** If a lukewarm launch won't torch your reputation or close the door on a second attempt, it's cheap to learn, adjust, and come back sharper.
- **You have a clear metric that tells you pass or fail.** "20 SMEs sign up for the waitlist in a month" is a real MVP success criterion, because it can actually be failed. "Let's see how it goes" is not. That's a full build wearing an MVP's name tag and hoping nobody checks.

## The mistake we see most often

It's usually not over-building. It's building an MVP with no plan whatsoever for what happens after it works. Founders scope the thinnest possible version, ship it, and it validates. Users show up, momentum builds, and then there's no architecture underneath it to grow into. The database was a spreadsheet. The "backend" was a form that quietly emailed someone. The very success everyone hoped for becomes the bottleneck, and now you're re-engineering under load with real customers watching.

The fix isn't to over-engineer the MVP to pre-empt a success you haven't earned yet. It's to be honest that the MVP validates the *idea*, and that a second, deliberate phase rebuilds the *foundation* once the idea has proven itself. Budget for both phases from day one, even if you only formally commit to phase one. That way the win doesn't catch you flat-footed.

## A practical way to scope it

Write the one-sentence question. Write the single metric that answers it. Then list every feature you're tempted to add, and cut anything that doesn't move that metric, including the features you're absolutely certain users will "obviously" want. Obvious-but-irrelevant is still irrelevant to *this* experiment. If a feature doesn't help answer your specific question, it belongs in phase two, not phase one, no matter how good it would feel to ship it now.
